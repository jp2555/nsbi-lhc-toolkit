Decoding the jargon

Lesson 1 threw a lot of vocabulary at once. Here's the same content rebuilt slowly, around one example — no equations until the end, and by then it reads itself.

Our running example. You want to measure how much H→ττ signal is in your data (call that amount μ: μ=1 means "exactly the Standard-Model amount"). The signal sits under backgrounds (Z→ττ, tt̄). One problem: the detector's τ energy calibration might be slightly off — say by ±1%. That small mis-calibration shifts your reconstructed mass spectra, so it could be faked as, or hide, signal. Everything below is about handling that.

Movement 1 — the setup

LikelihoodL — a number that says "how probable is the data I observed, if this hypothesis were true?" You tune the hypothesis (e.g. the value of μ) to make the data most probable. the whole measurement = find the μ that maximizes L, and ask how sharply L peaks (that's your error bar).
Likelihood ratior(x) — for a single event x, "how much more signal-like than background-like is it?" = (prob of x if signal) ÷ (prob of x if background). A classifier score, calibrated, is this ratio. it's the per-event evidence the whole fit is built from.
Binned vs unbinned (per-event) — Binned: histogram a variable (say mττ) and count events per bin (the classic "template" analysis). Unbinned / per-event: skip the histogram; use each event's own likelihood ratio. NSBI is the unbinned version. binning throws away information; per-event keeps it — that's the point of NSBI.

Movement 2 — systematics become "knobs"

Systematic uncertainty → nuisance parameterα — a systematic (the τ energy scale) is something you don't know exactly. You turn it into a knob α: α=0 is your best guess, α=+1 / −1 are the "1σ off" versions. The fit is allowed to turn this knob. "Nuisance" = you don't care about it, but you must account for it. this is the central move — every systematic is one (or more) of these α knobs.
Constraint termf(a|α) — the knob is springy: an auxiliary measurement says "α is near 0, give or take 1," so the fit pays a penalty for turning it far from 0. Usually a Gaussian pull. without it the fit could blame everything on systematics; the spring keeps them honest.
Anchor pointα(i) — a knob setting where you actually ran a full simulation. Typically you only have α = 0, +1, −1 simulated. you know the world at a few knob settings, but the fit needs every setting in between.

Movement 3 — the hard part

Morphing / interpolationΔ(x|α) — the recipe for "what does the data distribution look like at knob value α=0.37, when I only simulated 0 and ±1?" You morph (smoothly interpolate) the distribution between the anchor points. Δ(x|α) is exactly that deformation, event by event. this is the hard object of the whole field — it has no known formula, and α can be hundreds of knobs at once. Lesson 1's "five methods" are five ways to build Δ.
Surrogate — the true Δ(x|α) is intractable (you can't write it down or compute it exactly), so you train a neural network to stand in for it. The net is the surrogate. "learned surrogate for an intractable density ratio" = the one-line definition of NSBI.
Calibration — a classifier's raw output (0.83) is only useful if it's a truthful probability (events scoring 0.83 really are signal 83% of the time). Calibration (e.g. isotonic regression) fixes this. if the surrogate isn't calibrated, the fit is biased — Lesson 1 noted dropping it caused a 0.3–0.4 bias on μ.

Movement 4 — getting the answer

Profiling / profile likelihood — when you quote your result on μ, you let every nuisance knob α float to whatever value best fits the data (paying its spring penalty). "Profile over the nuisances" = optimize them away at each μ. this is how systematics "cost" you: floating the knobs widens your error bar on μ.
Test statistic & confidence intervaltμ — tμ = −2 × (log-likelihood drop) as you move μ away from its best value, after profiling. Your 68% confidence interval is the range of μ where tμ ≤ 1 (95% where ≤ 3.84). the number you ultimately report ("μ = 1.00 ± 0.17") comes straight from this curve. Smaller interval = better measurement.
Factorizable vs non-factorizable — Factorizable: each knob acts on its own, so the total effect is just the product of single-knob effects (the convenient assumption). Non-factorizable: knobs interact (turning JES changes what turning TES does). the newer methods (GP, refinable) can handle interacting knobs; older ones assume they don't interact.

Now re-read the Lesson 1 equation

P(x | η, χ) ∝ ∏events [ Σsamples ν · κ · Δ(x|α) · Ps(x) ] · ∏ constraints
SymbolIn plain words
∏ events"multiply over every event" — this is the per-event (unbinned) part
Σ samples"add up the processes" — signal + each background
ν, κhow many events each process gives (rates: luminosity, cross-section, the signal amount μ)
Δ(x|α)the morphing — how this event's probability bends as you turn the systematic knobs α (the hard, learned part)
Ps(x)the baseline per-event probability density for process s
∏ constraintsthe springs holding each knob α near its nominal value
η, χη = what you want (μ, κλ); χ = the nuisance knobs you profile away

Read aloud: "the probability of all the data = multiply over events, of (sum over processes of: how many × how-the-shape-bends-with-the-knobs × baseline density), times the springs on the knobs." That's the entire model.

The one win from this lesson: you can now read that equation in English, and you know that Δ(x|α) — the morphing — is the thing every method is fighting over.
